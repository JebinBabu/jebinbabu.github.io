The case for writing things down slowly

Most note-taking systems optimize for capture speed. I've started to think that's exactly backwards.

Every note-taking tool I've used in the last few years sells itself on the same promise: capture the thought before it disappears. Quick-add widgets, voice memos, one-tap templates. The entire design goal is to get the friction between having a thought and recording it as close to zero as possible.

I don't think that's wrong, exactly. But I've noticed that the notes I actually return to — the ones that still mean something months later — are almost never the fast ones. They're the ones I was slightly annoyed to be writing by hand, because writing by hand forced me to decide what the sentence was actually going to say before I started it.

Friction as a filter

A frictionless system captures everything, which means it captures the half-formed thought at the same fidelity as the fully-formed one. Later, sorting through it, you can't tell which is which — they all have the same clean formatting, the same timestamp, the same visual weight.

A slower system does some of that sorting for you, automatically, at the moment of writing. If a thought isn't worth the ten extra seconds it takes to phrase properly, it usually wasn't worth keeping either. The friction isn't a tax on capture — it's a first, cheap round of editing.

The point of a notebook isn't to lose nothing. It's to keep the right things.

Where this breaks down

This isn't an argument against speed everywhere. Fieldwork, interviews, anything happening in real time still needs fast capture, because the alternative is losing the material entirely. The argument is narrower: for reflective writing — the kind meant to be read again later — slow is doing real work, not just getting in the way.

So this notebook stays plain on purpose. No quick-capture button, no mobile shortcut, no voice-to-text. Just a page, opened deliberately, written once.
